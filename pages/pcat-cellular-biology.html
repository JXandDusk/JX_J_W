01 / STRUCTURE SUPPORTS FUNCTION

Cells and organelles

Cells are the basic living units of organisms. New cells arise from existing cells, and genetic information is passed on during reproduction. Membranes organize reactions and separate a cell from its surroundings.

Prokaryotic cells

Bacteria and Archaea lack a membrane-enclosed nucleus. Their DNA occupies a nucleoid region; they still have a membrane, cytoplasm, and ribosomes.

Eukaryotic cells

Have a membrane-enclosed nucleus and internal compartments. Animals, plants, fungi, and many other organisms are eukaryotes. Specialized cells can lose structures during development.

The nucleus supplies the genetic instructions; ribosomes synthesize the polypeptide; the ER and Golgi process and route it. The Golgi does not synthesize the protein.

02 / A SELECTIVE BOUNDARY

Membrane structure

Phospholipids have water-interacting heads and hydrophobic tails. In a bilayer, heads face the aqueous environments and tails face inward. Proteins contribute transport, recognition, anchoring, and signaling functions. The fluid mosaic model emphasizes a dynamic membrane, although movement can be constrained.

Predict permeability:

Small nonpolar molecules such as O₂ and CO₂ cross relatively readily. Ions require suitable transport proteins to cross efficiently. Water can cross the bilayer, but aquaporins often greatly increase its permeability. Being small does not make a charged solute freely membrane-permeable.

For ions, both concentration and electrical potential matter: use the electrochemical gradient. Symport means coupled solutes move in the same direction; antiport means opposite directions. These words describe direction, not by themselves the energy source.

Osmosis · Follow water

Water moves in both directions. Net osmosis reflects a water-potential difference across a selectively permeable membrane. In the simple equal-pressure case, impermeant solutes on one side promote net water movement toward that side.

Hypotonic outside

Lower effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute outside than inside. Net water entry can swell an animal cell and may cause lysis.

Isotonic outside

No sustained net volume change due to nonpenetrating solutes. Water continues exchanging; it does not stop moving.

Hypertonic outside

Higher effective concentration of nonpenetrating solute outside. Net water loss shrinks an animal cell. Plant plasmolysis specifically involves the protoplast withdrawing from the cell wall.

Practice example · Which way does water move?

A cell contains 300 mOsm/L of nonpenetrating solute and is placed in a 200 mOsm/L bath of nonpenetrating solute at equal pressure. The bath is hypotonic relative to the cell; net water enters initially.

If the added solute freely crosses the membrane, do not apply this impermeant-solute shortcut without considering equilibration. Osmolarity and tonicity are related but not interchangeable.

04 / SMALL-SCALE AND LARGE-SCALE MOVEMENT

Vesicles and circulation

Endocytosis

The plasma membrane encloses material and forms an internal vesicle. Phagocytosis captures large particles; pinocytosis takes up fluid; receptor-mediated pathways enrich selected cargo.

Exocytosis

A vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, adding membrane and releasing lumenal cargo outside. The cargo does not have to pass through the hydrophobic bilayer core.

Inside cells, thermal motion supports diffusion, while cytoplasmic streaming and motor-driven transport move material over longer intracellular distances. The ER is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane, but it is not an open channel directly connecting the nuclear envelope to the plasma membrane.

Across an organism, bulk circulation moves fluids over long distances, while diffusion remains essential for short-distance exchange between blood, interstitial fluid, and cells. A useful distinction is bulk delivery versus local exchange.

05 / COPY ONCE, DISTRIBUTE ACCURATELY

Cell cycle and mitosis

Interphase comprises G₁, S, and G₂. DNA is replicated during S phase. Mitosis partitions duplicated chromosomes; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. A duplicated chromosome has two sister chromatids until they separate.

G₁: growth→S: DNA replication→G₂: preparation→Mitosis + cytokinesis

Mitosis generally preserves the starting chromosome-set number. It is not restricted to diploid cells. In the standard diploid example, each daughter receives 2n chromosomes.

06 / SEPARATE HOMOLOGS, THEN SISTERS

Meiosis and chromosome counts

Homologous chromosomes carry corresponding loci and originate from different parents. Sister chromatids are the replicated copies of one chromosome, though recombination can make chromatids genetically different.

Meiosis I

Prophase I pairs homologs and permits crossing over between nonsister chromatids. Homologous pairs align in metaphase I. Anaphase I separates homologs while sisters generally remain together.

Meiosis II

No second S phase occurs. Sister chromatids separate in a division resembling mitosis. The usual model yields four haploid meiotic products; not every product becomes a functional gamete in every organism.

The counting rule that prevents mistakes

n describes chromosome sets. C describes DNA amount relative to one unreplicated haploid genome. Count chromosomes by centromeres; do not count the two arms of an X-shaped duplicated chromosome as two chromosomes.

During mitotic anaphase, separated chromatids are each counted as chromosomes, so an undivided human cell temporarily contains 92 daughter chromosomes in total, with 46 moving toward each pole. Specify whether you mean the whole cell, one pole, or one daughter nucleus.

Where variation and errors arise

Crossing over reshuffles linked alleles. Independent assortment reflects the orientation of different homologous pairs in meiosis I; it does not mean all genes assort independently. Nondisjunction is failed chromosome separation and can produce abnormal chromosome numbers.

07 / RETRIEVAL PRACTICE

Check your understanding

Answer before opening each explanation. These are original practice prompts, not questions copied from the book.

A cell secretes an enzyme. Which structures produce, process, and release it?

Ribosomes associated with rough ER make the polypeptide as it enters the secretory pathway. ER and Golgi participate in processing and sorting; vesicles deliver it for exocytosis. The Golgi is not the site of translation.

Glucose moves downhill through a carrier. Is that active transport?

No. Protein assistance alone does not make transport active. Downhill carrier-mediated movement can be facilitated diffusion.

Why can a red blood cell swell in a hypotonic bath?

Nonpenetrating solutes create an effective osmotic difference that drives net water entry. Lacking a rigid cell wall, the cell may lyse if swelling is excessive.

A human diploid cell finishes S phase. Does it now have 92 chromosomes?

No. It has 46 duplicated chromosomes and 92 chromatids. DNA amount has doubled to 4C, while chromosome-set number remains 2n.

What separates in anaphase I versus anaphase II?

Homologous chromosomes separate in anaphase I; sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.

A chromosome has two sister chromatids after meiosis I. Is the cell still diploid?

Not necessarily. Each standard meiosis-I product has one homolog from each pair and is haploid (n), while each chromosome remains duplicated (2C total DNA).

Does isotonic mean water has stopped crossing the membrane?

No. Exchange continues in both directions; there is no sustained net osmotic volume change in the stated conditions.

Why is “DNA replication makes a cell 4n” misleading?

It confuses chromosome sets with DNA content. For a conventional diploid cell, S phase changes 2n,2C to 2n,4C.
