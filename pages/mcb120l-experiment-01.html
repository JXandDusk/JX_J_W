Biological Buffers
Biological buffers

Learn how accurate pipetting, buffer preparation, and pH measurement establish and maintain the acid–base conditions needed for experiments.

Based on the Fall 2023 manual · Personal study notes

Purpose · Why do this experiment?

pH affects proteins, enzymes, and many other biomolecules. Molecular biology experiments therefore require suitable, stable acid–base conditions. Buffers help maintain those conditions.

This experiment connects three questions:How do we dispense accurately, prepare a target pH, and verify that we reached it? Calculate first, add reagents gradually, and verify with a pH meter. Then compare how different solutions respond to the same additions of acid or base.

Three useful terms

pH: A measure of acidity or alkalinity: lower values are more acidic and higher values more alkaline.
Buffer: A solution that reduces pH changes after small additions of acid or base; its capacity is limited.
Pipette: A tool for accurately measuring and transferring small liquid volumes.

What will you learn?

01 · Accurate dispensing

Add acid or base gradually with a pipette and record the actual volume.

02 · Preparing a target pH

Compare adding acid to a weak base, adding base to a weak acid, and mixing a conjugate acid–base pair.

03 · Measurement and verification

Compare measured and target pH values and observe buffer capacity.

Core principle · The ratio determines pH

Think of HA as the form with a releasable H⁺ and A as the form with one fewer H⁺. A is a general symbol, not necessarily a singly charged anion. A accepts added H⁺, while HA supplies H⁺ when base is added, reducing pH changes.

pH = pKₐ′ + log₁₀([A] / [HA])

[A] / [HA] is the concentration ratio of the two forms; pKₐ′ describes their acid–base equilibrium under the relevant conditions. A ratio of 1 gives log₁₀(1) = 0, so pH = pKₐ′. Ratios of 10 and 0.1 give pH values one unit above and below pKₐ′, respectively.

Understanding z, Kₐ, log Kₐ, and pH

These symbols connect changes in charge after H⁺ release, the tendency to release H⁺, and the resulting solution pH.

1 · z: the particle's net charge

z is the total molecular or ionic charge, not the number of atoms or released protons. The manual writes the general acid–base reaction as:

HAᶻ ⇌ H⁺ + Aᶻ⁻¹

HA is the protonated acid form and A its conjugate base. Losing one +1 H⁺ decreases the remaining particle's charge from z to z − 1. Total charge is conserved: z = (+1) + (z − 1).

Special case: the glycine zwitterion. H₃N⁺–CH₂–COO⁻ has both +1 and −1 charges, giving z = 0. After its ammonium group loses H⁺, H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ has net charge −1. Add all charges when determining z.

An acid need not be uncharged, and a base need not be negatively charged. HA and A describe their roles in this particular reaction.

2 · Kₐ: the equilibrium tendency to release H⁺

At equilibrium for HA ⇌ H⁺ + A, the acid dissociation constant is:

Kₐ = a(H⁺) × a(A) / a(HA)

a is activity—the effective participation of a species in solution. Rigorous calculations use dimensionless activities relative to a standard state. In sufficiently dilute, nearly ideal solutions, normalized concentrations approximate activities; a common teaching shorthand is:

Kₐ ≈ [H⁺][A] / [HA]

Square brackets denote concentration. A larger Kₐ indicates a greater tendency to release H⁺; a smaller Kₐ indicates a greater tendency to retain H⁺. Compare under identical conditions. Kₐ is neither reaction speed nor total acid concentration.

The manual also uses Kₐ′ (with a prime): Kₐ′ = a(H⁺)[A]/[HA]. This apparent constant incorporates activity-coefficient effects for A and HA and may vary with ionic strength and temperature. Use pKₐ′ for the relevant conditions.

3 · log Kₐ and pKₐ: comparing small numbers

Here log means base 10: what power of 10 gives this number?

Kₐlog₁₀ KₐpKₐ = −log₁₀ Kₐ
10⁻³ = 0.001−33
10⁻⁵ = 0.00001−55
10⁻⁷ = 0.0000001−77

Remember the minus sign: pKₐ is the negative of log Kₐ. Larger Kₐ means smaller pKₐ. A one-unit pKₐ difference corresponds to a tenfold Kₐ difference; pKₐ 3 gives a Kₐ 100 times that at pKₐ 5.

Using the manual's apparent acetic-acid value: pKₐ′ = 4.76 → log Kₐ′ = −4.76 → Kₐ′ = 10⁻⁴·⁷⁶ ≈ 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ (following the manual's concentration and standard-state conventions).

4 · pH: calculate from H⁺ or work backward
pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺)

In the dilute-solution approximation, a(H⁺) ≈ [H⁺]/(1 mol/L). This is often abbreviated pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺], using the numerical concentration in mol/L.

Example A:[H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L → pH ≈ −log₁₀(10⁻⁴) = 4.00.
Example B:[H⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L → pH ≈ −(log₁₀2 + log₁₀10⁻⁴) = −(0.301 − 4) = 3.70.

More H⁺ gives a lower pH. A one-unit pH decrease increases H⁺ activity tenfold.

Working backward: pH = 7.40 → a(H⁺) = 10⁻⁷·⁴⁰ ≈ 3.98 × 10⁻⁸. Under the dilute-solution approximation, [H⁺] ≈ 3.98 × 10⁻⁸ mol/L. This is H⁺ concentration, not the total concentration of a 0.1 M buffer.

5 · Deriving the buffer pH equation from Kₐ′

Start with the manual's apparent equilibrium relation:

  1. Kₐ′ = a(H⁺)[A]/[HA]
  2. Take log₁₀ of both sides, using log(xy) = log x + log y:
    log Kₐ′ = log a(H⁺) + log([A]/[HA])
  3. Substitute log Kₐ′ = −pKₐ′ and log a(H⁺) = −pH:
    −pKₐ′ = −pH + log([A]/[HA])
  4. Rearrange:
    pH = pKₐ′ + log([A]/[HA])

This is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, linking the acid's dissociation tendency and the ratio of the two forms to pH.

Common points of confusion

pH describes a particular solution; pKₐ′ describes an acid–base equilibrium under particular conditions. A 0.1 M buffer means [A] + [HA], which cannot be substituted directly into −log[H⁺]. HCl is a strong acid and cannot serve as HA in this weak-acid buffer relation.

Worked example · An acetate buffer at pH 4.0

Use the manual's pKₐ′ = 4.76 and a target of 100 mL, 0.1 M, pH 4.0. These are theoretical teaching calculations, not measured results, assuming the given pKₐ′ and neglecting activity effects and nonadditive volumes.

1 · Calculate the ratio

4.00 = 4.76 + log₁₀([A] / [HA])
log₁₀([A] / [HA]) = −0.76
[A] / [HA] = 10⁻⁰·⁷⁶ ≈ 0.174

For each part of acetic acid, use approximately 0.174 parts of acetate. The target pH is below pKₐ′, so the acid form predominates.

2 · Calculate the total buffer amount

n = C × V = 0.1 mol/L × 0.100 L = 0.010 mol = 10 mmol
n(A) + n(HA) = 10 mmol
n(A) = 10 × 0.174 / (1 + 0.174) ≈ 1.48 mmol
n(HA) ≈ 8.52 mmol

3 · Convert amounts into reagent volumes

Starting with 2.0 M acetic acid: 10 mmol ÷ 2.0 mmol/mL = 5.00 mL acetic-acid stock.
NaOH converts 1.48 mmol of acetic acid to acetate:
CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O
Here 0.2 N NaOH equals 0.2 mol/L, giving a theoretical volume of:
1.48 mmol ÷ 0.2 mmol/mL = 7.40 mL NaOH.

4 · Check by substitution

pH ≈ 4.76 + log₁₀(1.48 / 8.52) ≈ 4.00.

In practice, add base gradually, mix, and read pH. Use smaller additions near the target. Bring the total volume to 100 mL with water and measure again. The calculated volume is an estimate; measurement establishes the endpoint.

Another example · Why does Tris pH 9.0 need HCl?

The manual gives Tris pKₐ′ = 8.21.
[A] / [HA] = 10^(9.00 − 8.21) ≈ 6.17.

For 10 mmol total, HA ≈ 10/(1 + 6.17) = 1.39 mmol and base ≈ 8.61 mmol. Starting with pure Tris base, HCl converts approximately 1.39 mmol to the acid form.

Use 10.0 mL of 1.0 M Tris base; theoretical 1.0 N HCl volume ≈ 1.39 mL. Add water to about 80 mL, titrate and measure, then bring to 100 mL and remeasure. Tris pKₐ′ changes with temperature, so calculation does not replace measurement.

Why use a preparation table for phosphate?

The course table specifies 19.0 mL of 0.1 M KH₂PO₄ + 81.0 mL of 0.1 M K₂HPO₄ for pH 7.4. The ratio cannot reproduce that pH directly using dilute-solution pKₐ′ = 7.22; ionic strength and other conditions affect apparent equilibrium. Follow the course table and verify the measured pH.

Tris-HCl · Adjust pH with acid
  1. Calculate the 1.0 M Tris base, titrant, and water required for 100 mL of 0.1 M buffer.
  2. Add Tris base to a beaker and add deionized water to about 80 mL, leaving space for titrant.
  3. Use a P-1000 to add 1.0 N HCl gradually, mixing and measuring to pH 9.0. Record the actual acid volume.
  4. Use a graduated cylinder to bring the volume to 100 mL; remeasure and record final pH.
Acetate · Adjust pH with base
  1. Calculate the 2.0 M acetic acid, titrant, and water required for 100 mL of 0.1 M buffer.
  2. Add acetic acid to a beaker and add deionized water to about 20 mL.
  3. Use a P-1000 to add 0.2 N NaOH gradually, mixing and measuring to pH 4.0. Record the actual base volume.
  4. Use a graduated cylinder to bring the volume to 100 mL; remeasure and record final pH.
Phosphate · Mix two phosphate salts
  1. Following Table A-1, measure 19.0 mL of 0.1 M KH₂PO₄ and 81.0 mL of 0.1 M K₂HPO₄.
  2. Combine in a beaker and mix with a magnetic stir bar.
  3. Measure the actual pH and compare it with the 7.4 target.
Important measurement details

The manual calls for continuous stirring during titration but stopping the stirrer for an accurate pH reading. Keep the stir bar from striking the electrode. Remeasure after adjusting final volume; do not report the target as the measured result.

Use a pipette for small precise additions and a graduated cylinder for the 100 mL final volume. The manual does not provide full pipette training; consult course introductory material and instrument instructions.

Further experiments · From pH to buffer capacity

B · How does dilution affect buffer capacity?

Prepare 10 mL each of undiluted, 1/2-diluted, and 1/10-diluted 0.10 M Tris buffer. Calibrate the pH meter over pH 7–10 as instructed and record each pH.

To separate 4 mL portions add 1 mL of 0.05 N HCl or 0.05 M NaOH. Mix and measure. Compare pH changes at different buffer concentrations for identical acid or base additions.

C · How does pH alter an indicator's spectrum?

Mix 20 μL of 0.1% (w/v) bromophenol blue with 980 μL of either 0.1 N HCl or 0.1 M Tris at pH 9, giving a 1/50 dilution. Scan 800–400 nm in plastic cuvettes and compare peak wavelengths and absorbances.

D · Estimate buffer pH using p-nitrophenol

First mix 100 μL of 0.5 mM p-nitrophenol with 900 μL of 0.1 N HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Compare acid and base spectra in quartz cuvettes.

Prepare two 1/2 serial dilution series. Each plate well receives 225 μL of NaOH or the relevant buffer plus 25 μL of diluted dye; dye-free blanks receive 25 μL water. Final dye dilutions are 1/10, 1/20, 1/40, 1/80, and 1/160.

Read A410 and estimate pH using pKₐ 7.15 and Henderson–Hasselbalch. Compare with electrode readings. The estimate has a limited valid range and is not equally reliable for every buffer.
