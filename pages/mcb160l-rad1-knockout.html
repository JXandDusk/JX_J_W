RAD1 Knockout

Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Organized learning notes based on my course notebook, with recorded observations distinguished from interpretation.

Purpose

Use reverse genetics to connect a known DNA-repair gene to a measurable phenotype. Construct a deletion cassette, select transformants, and test whether molecular evidence and UV-response evidence agree.

Project details · Why knock out RAD1?

A knockout asks whether a gene is required for a particular function. Instead of only comparing unrelated strains, we deliberately change RAD1 in a parental yeast background and compare the resulting strain with appropriate controls. The project tests a link between RAD1-dependent repair and survival after UV damage.

Prediction: a correctly disrupted RAD1 strain is expected to be more UV-sensitive than the parental strain under matched exposure. The exact amount of growth depends on exposure and strain conditions; knockout does not mean cells must fail to grow under every condition.

What is replaced, and why use HIS3?

The cassette includes HIS3 between regions homologous to the RAD1 locus. Homologous recombination can place this cassette into the intended genomic region. In the starting histidine-auxotrophic strain, a functional HIS3 marker permits growth on medium without added histidine. That selects candidates, including possible ectopic integrations, for further testing.

Reading the course junction-PCR gel

The reported primers bind approximately 100 bp into the upstream flank and 400 bp into HIS3. Thus a product near 500 bp is expected for the intended junction. The parental strain should not give that same target-junction product because HIS3 is not at that location. An unrelated amplifiable control is needed to interpret a negative lane.

How to read the UV plates

Compare each strain’s unexposed and exposed growth under the same plating/input conditions. Unexposed growth establishes baseline viability. A candidate that grows normally without UV but poorly after UV, compared with a less-sensitive parental strain, is consistent with the expected phenotype. Poor growth in both conditions could reflect a general viability or handling problem.

How to read an agarose gel

Read three things separately: band position = approximate fragment size; band brightness = amount of stained DNA under comparable conditions; band number = number of resolved DNA species. None of these alone establishes a successful experiment.

  1. Start with the lane map: identify the ladder, wild-type/positive sample, experimental sample, and no-template or other negative control.
  2. Locate the wells. Negatively charged DNA travels toward the positive electrode. For comparable linear DNA fragments, shorter products generally travel farther from the wells.
  3. Compare the target band with nearby ladder bands. The spacing is not linear in base pairs; use known ladder sizes and, when needed, a calibration of log₁₀(bp) against migration distance.
  4. Compare observed products with the prediction from the primer or restriction map. A band near the expected size is consistent with the prediction but is not sequence confirmation.
  5. Check controls before interpreting missing or extra products. A failed positive control makes a negative sample result inconclusive; a target-sized band in the no-template control suggests contamination or another assay artifact.
AppearancePossible explanationsWhat to check next
Single expected-size bandLikely dominant product of the expected lengthControls, specificity, and sequence/independent verification

An undigested plasmid can produce multiple bands because supercoiled, nicked/open-circle, and other forms migrate differently. Do not estimate its size as if every band were linear DNA. In a digest, equal-length fragments can merge into one band and very short fragments may not be visible, so visible band count can differ from predicted fragment count.

Core principle

A HIS3 cassette with RAD1-flanking homology can replace the target region through homologous recombination. HIS3 selection tests whether cells can grow without supplied histidine; it does not establish where the cassette integrated. Junction PCR and phenotype assays answer different questions.

Workflow · What each stage contributes

  1. Plan homology arms and PCR primers for the HIS3 cassette.
  2. Amplify the cassette and check it against a no-template control on an agarose gel.
  3. Transform yeast and select candidate HIS3-positive colonies.
  4. Use locus/cassette-specific PCR to test the intended integration.
  5. Compare wild-type, mutant, and candidate colonies under matched UV-exposure and unexposed conditions.

Worked calculation · Loading buffer

For 10 µL PCR product plus 5 µL water, add V µL of 5× loading buffer to obtain 1× final:

5V = 15 + V → V = 3.75 µL

The final sample volume is 18.75 µL. This calculation includes the volume contributed by the loading buffer.

Recorded observations & interpretation

The notebook reports choosing the 1/10-template PCR preparation for transformation after comparing gel results. During the verification gel, the wild-type sample was lost during loading. The candidate lane was interpreted as containing an approximately 500-bp junction product. The recorded UV assay showed reduced growth of the restreaked candidates after exposure.

What the evidence can and cannot establish

Reduced UV survival is consistent with impaired repair, but cannot by itself prove the intended deletion. The lost wild-type verification lane limits that gel comparison. An expected junction band supports integration; an independent assay for absence of the intact locus would strengthen a deletion claim. RAD1 loss does not remove every DNA-repair pathway.
