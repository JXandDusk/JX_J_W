Protein concentration determination

Use light and known standards to determine how much protein an unknown solution contains.

Purpose · Why measure protein concentration?

Two protein solutions may look identical but contain different protein amounts. Before comparing samples, planning reactions, or controlling protein loading, determine concentration.

Use known BSA (bovine serum albumin) standards to measure unknown BSA concentration. Compare responses of different proteins to understand why methods may disagree. Repeat the Bradford assay to practice pipetting, dilution, and analysis.

01 · Interpret the signal

Absorbance is an instrument signal, not concentration itself.

02 · Establish a reference

Convert signal to concentration using a standard curve.

03 · Check reliability

Subtract blanks, use replicates, check the range, and recover stock concentration.

Visual guide · How does light reveal concentration?

A = log₁₀(I₀ / I) = −log₁₀(I / I₀)

A is dimensionless absorbance. If 10% of incident light is transmitted, A = −log₁₀(0.10) = 1.00. Within the linear range, Beer–Lambert gives:

A = εlc or A = alc

ε is the molar absorption coefficient used with mol/L; a is the mass-based coefficient used here with mg/mL; l is in cm. Do not mix coefficients and concentration units. Example: For a = 0.67 (mg/mL)⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l = 1.00 cm, and corrected A = 0.335, c = A/(al) = 0.500 mg/mL. These are hypothetical teaching values, not personal measurements.

Key point 1 · Subtract the blank first

Blank Contains background components such as water, buffer, or color reagent, but no analyte protein. It measures the signal produced without protein.

Acorrected = Asample − Ablank

Teaching example: sample A595 = 0.42 and blank A595 = 0.06, so corrected absorbance = 0.36. Do not subtract the same background twice if the instrument already blank-corrected it. Treat standards and unknowns consistently.

Key point 2 · Determine concentration from a standard curve

For a validated linear curve y = mx + b, unknown concentration x = (y − b)/m. Example: If y = 0.020x with x in μg/mL, y = 0.36 gives x = 18 μg/mL. For a curved response, use a suitable fit or interpolation within range, rather than forcing a straight line.

Use reliable signals within the standard range

Dilute high-reading unknowns and adjust low-reading samples. Avoid blind extrapolation. The manual warns that absorbance around 1.5–2.0 and flattened color responses may be unreliable; limits depend on instrument and assay.

Key point 3 · Serial dilution and stock concentration

Stock concentration = concentration from curve × dilution factor

If 18 μg/mL came from a 1/27 dilution, stock concentration = 18 × 27 = 486 μg/mL = 0.486 mg/mL.

Should the reagent dilution add another factor of 2?

The x axis in manual Table 2.4 uses sample concentration before reagent addition. Standards and unknowns both receive 125 μL sample + 125 μL reagent, so the curve already returns pre-reagent concentration:do not multiply by 2 again. If you instead define the x axis as final well concentration, account separately for the 1:1 reagent dilution. Check the axis definition first.

Experimental steps · Essential parameters

Part A · Unknown BSA by A280
  1. Compare UV spectra of 0.80 mg/mL BSA, 0.40 mg/mL lysozyme, and 2.0 mg/mL gelatin in quartz cuvettes. Record peaks, A280, and A260. Their concentrations differ, so peak height alone does not compare sensitivity.
  2. Prepare duplicate 1 mL standards from 2.0 mg/mL BSA: 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, and 1.50 mg/mL.
  3. Measure undiluted unknown plus duplicate 1/2 and 1/4 dilutions.
  4. Read A280, handle blanks, construct a standard curve, and calculate stock concentration using valid readings and the appropriate dilution factor.

Dilution example: 2.0 mg/mL × V₁ = 0.50 mg/mL × 1.00 mL → V₁ = 0.250 mL, or 250 μL stock + 750 μL water.

Part B · Bradford microplate assay
  1. Prepare two 1 mL tubes of 100 μg/mL BSA, each with 50 μL of 2.0 mg/mL stock + 950 μL water.
  2. Prepare two standard sets and two 1/3 serial dilution series of the unknown.
  3. Add 125 μL standard or unknown per well, then 125 μL Bradford reagent after all samples are placed. Mix, avoid bubbles, and keep reaction times consistent.
  4. Read A595 using the course procedure. Place standards and unknowns on the same plate; repeated readings of one plate do not create independent replicates.
  5. Average replicates, subtract blank, select an appropriate curve, and use within-range unknown readings to recover stock concentration.

96-well plate · Where does each sample go?

A1, A2: Protein-free blank. B1–H1 and B2–H2: 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 μg/mL standards before reagent addition. A3–H3 and A4–H4: Unknown dilutions from 1/3 to 1/6561. Each well receives 125 μL sample + 125 μL Bradford reagent, totaling 250 μL.

The result matches the assumed true concentration. Here multiply only by 27, not by another 2, because the x axis already uses pre-reagent sample concentration and standards and unknowns receive the same equal-volume reagent addition.

For a 1/81 dilution, assume mean A595 = 0.180; blank correction gives 0.120. Curve: 0.120/0.020 = 6.0 μg/mL; multiply by 81 → 486 μg/mL, recovering the same stock concentration. Valid dilutions should agree approximately; investigate mixing, pipetting, and curve range if they do not.

Part C · Why do equal protein concentrations give different signals?

Compare BSA, lysozyme, and gelatin by A280 and Bradford. Amino-acid composition and dye-binding properties differ, so equal mass concentrations need not produce equal signals.

For Bradford comparison, start with 0.1 mg/mL protein stocks: mix 125 μL stock + 375 μL water or 250 μL stock + 250 μL water. After adding equal reagent volume to 125 μL sample, final well concentrations are 0.0125 and 0.025 μg/μL. These are final well concentrations, unlike the earlier standard-curve x axis.

Sensitivity is signal change per concentration change, not accuracy. Compare corrected absorbance with concentration, path length, and valid response range accounted for. A microplate path length is not automatically 1 cm.

Part D · Check technique with a known sample

Repeat Part B2 with a sample whose true concentration is provided. Check standards, pipetting, dilution factors, and analysis against the known value.

Using the true concentration as reference, percent error is:|measured concentration − true concentration| / true concentration × 100%. For 0.50 mg/mL true and 0.54 mg/mL measured, error = 8%. This is a teaching example; use the course definition if its “% difference” differs.

Checks · When should you repeat the assay?

  • Standard-curve R² < 0.95: The manual requires repeating the assay. High R² alone does not guarantee accuracy; also check replicates and curve range.
  • Irregular unknown dilution series or no reliable within-range reading: Adjust dilution and repeat.
  • Part D differs from the known concentration by more than 10%: Check calculations, fit, and dilution factor first. If correct, prepare fresh standards and samples and repeat.
  • Interfering substances: Choose a method compatible with sample composition. Blank subtraction compensates only for additive background; it cannot fix every chemical interference.

Supplementary method · BCA protein assay

Independent supplement using the standard Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit microplate protocol. This is not a procedure in the original course manual and is not presented as an experiment personally completed in class.

Purpose and principle

Protein-mediated copper reduction followed by BCA binding to Cu⁺ creates a purple signal. Relate A562 to protein concentration: protein → Cu²⁺ reduced to Cu⁺ → purple BCA–Cu⁺ complex → read at 562 nm.

Confirm the kit first

These conditions apply to standard Pierce BCA, not Micro BCA or Rapid Gold. Reducing and chelating agents interfere; check the specific kit's compatibility guidance.

Microplate procedure

  1. Prepare duplicate or greater BSA standards: 0 (blank), 25, 125, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1500, and 2000 μg/mL. Match standard and unknown diluents where possible.
  2. Prepare working reagent at A:B = 50:1 by volume.
  3. Add 25 μL standard or unknown plus 200 μL working reagent per well; shake for 30 seconds.
  4. Cover and incubate at 37°C for 30 minutes. Cool to room temperature and read A562.
  5. Subtract the mean blank and construct a curve using pre-reagent standard concentrations. Use within-range unknowns. Working range is 20–2000 μg/mL; do not assume strict linearity throughout.

BCA color continues developing; keep standard and sample processing and reading times consistent.

Method source:Thermo Fisher · Official Pierce BCA instructions (MAN0011430, Rev. D).

Suggested plate layout · Teaching design

On a separate plate, place the nine ascending standards in A1–A9 and B1–B9, duplicate 1/2 unknown dilutions in C1/D1, and duplicate 1/4 dilutions in C2/D2. Remaining wells are spare. This supplemental layout is separate from the course Bradford plate.

Preparation example · Standards and working reagent

For 500 μL of 500 μg/mL standard from 2 mg/mL BSA: V₁ = (500 × 500)/2000 = 125 μL stock + 375 μL diluent. Apply C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ to other standards.

The layout uses 22 wells: 18 standards + 4 unknowns. Minimum working reagent = 22 × 200 μL = 4.40 mL. Allow pipetting excess: 5.00 mL A + 0.100 mL B gives 5.10 mL at 50:1. This is a preparation example.

Worked example · Recovering 1.00 mg/mL

Hypothetical data: true stock = 1.00 mg/mL. Mix 50 μL sample + 50 μL diluent for a 1/2 dilution. Infer the stock from simulated readings before checking the answer.

Assume the local range is validated as y = 0.000700x with x in μg/mL. This table illustrates a local relationship; real analysis requires the full standards and an appropriate fit.

  1. Unknown duplicates: 0.424 and 0.436; mean = 0.430.
  2. Subtract blank: 0.430 − 0.080 = 0.350.
  3. Use curve: x = 0.350/0.000700 = 500 μg/mL.
  4. Correct the 1/2 dilution: 500 × 2 = 1000 μg/mL = 1.00 mg/mL.

Do not multiply by 9 again: 25 μL sample + 200 μL working reagent reduces final well concentration ninefold. Standards and unknowns share this step, and the x axis uses pre-reagent concentrations, so the curve already accounts for it. Correct only the prior 1/2 dilution.

For a 1/4 dilution with mean A562 = 0.255, corrected A = 0.175 gives 250 μg/mL; multiplying by 4 again gives 1.00 mg/mL. Agreement across valid dilutions checks consistency.
