Meiosis & Embryo Viability

Caenorhabditis elegans

Organized learning notes based on my course notebook, with recorded observations distinguished from interpretation.

Purpose

Compare chromosome organization in oocytes and offspring viability to investigate a possible meiotic defect using two complementary readouts.

Skills Gained · Workplace applications

Fluorescence microscopy

Skill practiced: Identify relevant anatomy and focus through a three-dimensional specimen.

Workplace application: Acquire interpretable cell-level observations.

Quantitative phenotyping

Skill practiced: Calculate viability proportions from consistent counts.

Workplace application: Compare samples with different total offspring numbers.

Statistical interpretation

Skill practiced: Distinguish a nonsignificant test from evidence of equality.

Workplace application: Report uncertainty and reconcile multiple readouts.

Project details · Why measure both chromosomes and offspring?

A meiotic defect may change chromosome pairing, segregation, or later embryo viability. Chromosome-body counts provide a cellular snapshot; offspring lethality provides an outcome across development. The readouts complement each other and need not show the same magnitude of change.

How to read the fluorescence images

Identify the correct late-prophase oocytes near the spermatheca. Focus through the full cell: two objects can overlap in one optical plane. Normally six bivalents are expected in XX worms. Extra resolved bodies can be compatible with unpaired chromosomes, but overlapping bodies, poor focus, or scoring mistakes can change the count. Fewer visible spots do not automatically mean chromosomes were deleted.

The notebook contains outlying counts, including values above the expected univalent count for a normal diploid oocyte. Those observations should be revisited for cell-stage or segmentation errors; defining everything outside mean ± one standard deviation as an “outlier” is not an adequate exclusion rule.

How to read progeny counts

Remove the laying adults to define a cohort, allow adequate time for hatching, then compare unhatched embryos with total observed offspring. Normalize by the total because different plates can have different offspring numbers.

More unhatched embryos supports lower viability only when cohort age, temperature, handling, and denominator are comparable. A significant lethality difference does not uniquely identify the molecular lesion.

What the recorded results support

The high-lethality strain differed strongly in the reported class viability summaries, while the reported chromosome-count test was nonsignificant. This supports a viability defect under these conditions, not proof of identical chromosome pairing or of one particular mutated protein. Account for oocytes nested within worms and plates as biological sampling units. There is no agarose-gel interpretation for these microscopy and viability measurements.

Core principle

Fluorescent chromosome reporters allow late-prophase oocyte DNA bodies to be counted. Normally paired homologs form six bivalents in XX C. elegans. Hatch/viability measurements test a downstream outcome. Similar chromosome-body counts do not guarantee normal segregation.

Workflow · What each stage contributes

  1. Orient the worm and identify oocytes near the spermatheca.
  2. Focus through the oocyte to count all fluorescent chromosome bodies.
  3. Record individual observations with worm and strain identifiers.
  4. Remove the laying adults before following the existing offspring cohort.
  5. Count live progeny and unhatched embryos, calculate lethality, and compare strains.

Recorded-count example · Embryo lethality

One recorded plate contained 235 unhatched embryos and 41 live progeny:

Lethality = 235/(235 + 41) ≈ 0.851 = 85.1%

The paired notebook count was 6 unhatched and 157 live: 6/163 ≈ 3.68%. These are plate-level observations, not class means.

Recorded observations & interpretation

Reported class summaries: FM656 chromosome-body count mean 6.145 (n = 55), FM668 mean 6.523 (n = 44), with a reported p = 0.218. Reported lethality means were 0.0478 and 0.822 respectively (20 class observations per strain), with a reported p < 0.0001. The notebook identifies FM656 as the wild-type-like strain and FM668 as the high-lethality strain.

What the evidence can and cannot establish

The p-values are reported from the notebook, not independently regenerated from a complete raw dataset. Its first null hypothesis should concern a difference in mean chromosome counts, not lethality. Its t-statistic formula and degrees-of-freedom convention mix Welch and pooled assumptions; a fresh analysis should use one consistent method. Oocytes nested within worms may not be independent biological replicates. No significant count difference does not establish equal meiosis, and these observations do not uniquely identify a mutated gene. The two supplied meiosis files have identical extracted text and are combined here.

Review questions

  • Why remove adults before the viability count?
  • Why compare proportions instead of unhatched counts alone?
  • What could explain high lethality without a clear count difference?
