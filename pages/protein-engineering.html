Chemistry & amino acids

Electrons and bonding

The octet rule is a useful main-group bonding model: atoms often gain, lose, or share electrons to complete a valence shell. It is not a rule that two atoms must have eight electrons in total; hydrogen follows a duet.

Electronegativity describes attraction for bonding electrons. Unequal sharing produces partial charges and polar bonds.

Hydrogen bonds

A donor carries a hydrogen bonded to an electronegative atom; an acceptor provides an available lone pair. Protein backbone groups, polar side chains, and water can participate.

Geometry matters: donor-H-acceptor arrangements generally favor near-linear alignment. Actual geometry reflects the surrounding structure.

pH, pKa, and pI: three different ideas

TermMeaningUse
pHAcidity of the solution; pH = -log₁₀ a(H⁺)Defines the environment experienced by the protein.
pKaAcid dissociation tendency of a particular groupPredicts protonation. Local protein environments can shift it.
pIpH at which average net charge is zeroHelps reason about charge-based separation; zero net charge does not mean no charged groups.

For a simple acid/base pair, pH below pKa favors the protonated form; pH above pKa favors the deprotonated form. Above a protein's pI it is typically net negative, and below its pI net positive.

Worked example · Protonation of an acidic side chain

pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA])

Assume pKa = 4.0 and pH = 7.0. The ratio [A⁻]/[HA] = 10³ = 1,000. The deprotonated fraction is 1,000 / 1,001 ≈ 99.9%. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured residue-specific pKa.

Interactions & folding energetics

ΔG = ΔH - TΔS

Compare free-energy changes between states, rather than treating the absolute value of G as a test of favorability. Negative ΔG favors the stated change under the stated conditions. T is absolute temperature in kelvin, and ΔS includes the protein and its environment.

InteractionPhysical ideaEngineering question
Hydrophobic effectBurial of nonpolar surface changes hydration; solvent entropy is important, with enthalpic contributions depending on conditions.Will a mutation improve core packing or expose a hydrophobic patch?
Hydrogen bondingDirectional interactions between donors and acceptors, competing with interactions with solvent.Does a substitution leave a buried polar group unsatisfied?
Electrostatic interactionsCharged and polar groups interact; strength depends on geometry, dielectric environment, and screening.How might pH or salt alter an interaction?
Van der Waals interactionsAttraction at suitable distances; steep repulsion at very short distances.Does a larger side chain introduce a clash?
Disulfide bondsCovalent links between cysteine residues.Is the expression environment compatible with the desired disulfide pattern?
Lennard-Jones potential · Why distance matters

E(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² - (σ/r)⁶]

r is separation, ε is well depth, and σ is the distance where the potential is zero. The minimum occurs at r = 2¹/⁶σ. The 12th-power term models short-range repulsion; the 6th-power term models attraction. This standard expression clarifies the ambiguous fraction in the original notes.

A salt bridge does not automatically stabilize a protein: favorable interactions must be balanced against desolvation and other energetic costs. Folding also reduces chain conformational entropy.

Structure & molecular geometry

LevelWhat it describes
PrimaryAmino-acid sequence.
SecondaryLocal backbone patterns, including α-helices, β-sheets, and turns.
TertiaryOverall three-dimensional arrangement of one polypeptide chain.
QuaternaryArrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits in an assembly.

A peptide bond has partial double-bond character and is approximately planar. Backbone conformation is described largely by φ (rotation about N-Cα) and ψ (about Cα-C′). The peptide-bond torsion ω is usually near 180° in the trans configuration, with exceptions.

α-helices

Typical backbone hydrogen bonds link the carbonyl of residue i to the N-H of i+4. Aligned peptide dipoles give the helix a partial positive end near its N terminus and a partial negative end near its C terminus.

β-sheets and turns

Hydrogen bonds connect neighboring strands in parallel or antiparallel sheets. Turns and loops connect structural elements. Additional patterns include 3₁₀ helices and polyproline conformations.

Ramachandran plots

Plot φ on the horizontal axis and ψ on the vertical axis. Clusters reflect frequently observed backbone conformations. Glycine allows a broader range; proline is more restricted.

Illustrative regionφ / ψ, approximately
Right-handed α-helix-60° / -45°
Extended β-strand-120° / +120°
Left-handed α-helix+60° / +45°

These are representative values, not exact boundaries or a classifier for every residue. The original β-sheet table conflicts with its own diagram; the extended β region belongs in the upper-left quadrant.

Rotamers, motifs, and structure interpretation

Rotamers are preferred side-chain conformations described by χ torsion angles. They matter for active sites, packing, and binding. X-ray models may include alternate conformations; an NMR ensemble reflects restraints and uncertainty and should not automatically be read as a time-resolved movie.

Recurring architectures in the notes include helix bundles, β-propellers, Rossmann folds, horseshoe-shaped arrangements, and β-barrels. A motif is a recurring pattern; a domain is a larger structural unit and is not simply another word for a motif.

X-ray crystallography: from diffraction to a model

Purify→Crystallize→Collect diffraction→Process & phase→Build, refine & validate

Crystallization

Screen protein concentration, pH, salts, and precipitants to find conditions supporting ordered crystals. In hanging-drop vapor diffusion, a protein/precipitant drop equilibrates with a reservoir of lower water activity, typically concentrating the drop as water leaves. Sitting-drop setups use the same general principle; batch trials mix components directly.

Bragg's law

nλ = 2d sin θ

d is lattice-plane spacing, λ is wavelength, n is diffraction order, and θ is the Bragg angle. The detector scattering angle is 2θ.

Worked example · Diffraction angle

For n = 1, λ = 1.00 Å, and d = 2.00 Å: sin θ = 1/(2 × 2) = 0.25. Therefore θ ≈ 14.5° and 2θ ≈ 29.0°. This is a teaching example.

What the diffraction pattern provides

The atomic scattering factor describes how an atom's electrons scatter X-rays. Contributions from atoms combine into a complex structure factor, carrying amplitude and phase. Measured intensities relate to squared amplitudes; phases must be estimated, for example by molecular replacement or experimental phasing.

A Fourier relationship connects structure factors and electron density. A density map guides model building; refinement fits observations while restraining sensible molecular geometry. A map is evidence supporting a model, not a photograph of individual atoms.

How to assess a structural model

QuantityInterpretation
ResolutionSmaller Å values usually permit finer detail; local support can still vary.
R-work / R-freeAgreement with reflections used in refinement / a held-out set. Their difference can flag overfitting. No universal 20% cutoff establishes quality.
B-factorRepresents modeled displacement/disorder and other effects; high values require context.
Geometry and mapsCheck clashes, backbone/side-chain conformations, and agreement with density.
B-factor calculation · Supplementary explanation

For an isotropic displacement model: B = 8π²Uiso. If B = 20 Å², Uiso ≈ 0.253 Å² and √Uiso ≈ 0.503 Å. Uiso is the mean-square displacement along one Cartesian direction; the three-dimensional mean-square displacement is 3Uiso. This model-based interpretation is not a direct measurement of a unique motion.

Reading structural files · Supplementary explanation

In a traditional PDB ATOM record, identify atom name, residue name, chain ID, residue number, x/y/z coordinates, occupancy, and temperature factor. Review experimental method, resolution, and validation information too. PDBx/mmCIF is the modern archive format. Do not interpret a computed model's confidence field as an experimental thermal B-factor.

NMR & electron microscopy

The source lists several learning objectives here without full explanations. The following is a concise conceptual completion, separate from experimental results.

MethodEvidence and strengthsKey limitations
X-ray crystallographyDiffraction from crystals supports electron-density maps and atomic models.Requires suitable crystals; disorder and crystal packing complicate interpretation.
Solution NMRNuclear magnetic measurements provide restraints and information about solution behavior.Sample preparation, spectral overlap, and molecular size affect feasibility.
Single-particle cryo-EMMany particle projections support reconstruction and classification of structures.Grid quality, heterogeneity, size, and image processing affect achievable detail.
Electron tomographyA tilt series of a specimen supports a three-dimensional reconstruction, often in a cellular context.Limited tilt range, dose, and specimen thickness constrain the reconstruction.
NMR signals: what each measurement contributes
  • Chemical shifts: local electronic environments; informative about conformation.
  • J coupling: through-bond interactions; selected couplings constrain torsion angles.
  • NOE: through-space effects that can support short-distance restraints.

Combine restraints to calculate and assess structures. Choosing NMR does not require failed crystallization; the scientific question may specifically concern dynamics or disordered regions.

Method choice is not governed by fixed 40 or 75 kDa cutoffs. Consider the question, sample behavior, assembly size, available equipment, and achievable evidence. Single-particle cryo-EM and tomography are distinct workflows.

Protein production & purification

DNA → RNA → Protein

Sequence design and an expression construct connect molecular biology to protein production. Select a host, regulatory elements, tag, and optional cleavage site appropriate for the target and downstream use.

PCR: amplify the DNA input

A PCR reaction needs template, two primers, dNTPs, thermostable polymerase, and suitable buffer/Mg²⁺. Cycles alternate denaturation, annealing, and extension. Primers anneal to opposite template strands with their 3′ ends pointing into the target; polymerase extends DNA 5′→3′.

Temperature and cycle count depend on enzyme and primer design. Thirty cycles do not guarantee saturation or success. Unexpected products can reflect primer specificity, annealing conditions, or contamination; missing products can reflect template quality or reaction setup.

Choose a separation principle

PropertyTechniqueWhat to remember
Specific bindingAffinity chromatographyCapture through a tag or ligand; evaluate nonspecific binding.
Net chargeIon exchangeChoose pH and resin using expected charge; salt can alter binding.
Hydrodynamic sizeSize-exclusion chromatographyLarger species generally elute earlier; shape also affects behavior.
SolubilitySalt precipitationFractionate by salt-dependent precipitation.
HydrophobicityHydrophobic interaction / reverse phaseThese use different conditions; reverse phase can be denaturing.
Electrophoretic behaviorSDS-PAGE / native PAGE / isoelectric focusingSDS-PAGE estimates mass; native mobility depends on several properties; IEF separates by pI.

Measure the right kind of quality

Concentration

A280, Bradford (595 nm), and conventional BCA (562 nm) estimate protein amount. They do not establish purity on their own.

Purity and identity

SDS-PAGE assesses band composition; tag/antibody detection supports target presence. Mass spectrometry provides mass or peptide evidence for identity.

Function and homogeneity

Activity assays address function. SEC and DLS can help assess aggregation or sample heterogeneity; interpret each measurement within its limitations.

Protease cleavage can remove a purification tag if it interferes with downstream work. Cleavage does not automatically restore a fully native protein: residual sequence, folding, and activity must be checked. Separate cleaved tag, protease, and uncleaved material as appropriate.

07 / LG 46 · SOURCE PP. 36-38

An integrated protein-to-structure workflow

  1. Define the question. Specify the target, desired function, and evidence needed.
  2. Design the construct. Choose sequence boundaries, vector, tag, and cleavage site; verify the DNA.
  3. Choose expression conditions. Consider folding, modifications, yield, and host compatibility.
  4. Recover and capture. Lyse cells and use a suitable initial purification step.
  5. Remove tags when needed. Check cleavage and separate remaining components.
  6. Polish the sample. Use complementary separation properties to improve purity and homogeneity.
  7. Check quality. Establish concentration, purity, identity, aggregation state, and relevant activity.
  8. Choose a structural route. Crystallize for X-ray work, prepare grids for cryo-EM, or prepare suitable NMR samples.
  9. Analyze and validate. Evaluate data quality, model support, uncertainty, and agreement with biochemical evidence.
Planning example · A soluble enzyme

Suppose the goal is to study a purified enzyme's structure and activity. A possible sequence is construct verification → expression screen → affinity capture → optional tag cleavage → SEC → gel/identity/activity checks → structural-method feasibility trials.

Why this order? Capture reduces mixture complexity; tag removal precedes final polishing; sample checks prevent investing in structural data collection with an unsuitable sample. This is a hypothetical planning exercise, not a report of work performed.

Skills this material develops

Reasoning about charge and stability; interpreting structural geometry; comparing purification principles; reading structural-quality evidence; and planning a workflow with explicit checks. Course knowledge supports these skills but does not, by itself, demonstrate hands-on proficiency in every method.

08 / REVIEW & CLARIFICATIONS

What to keep clear

Corrections to shorthand in the original notes
  • Use ΔG for favorability of a change, not an absolute G value.
  • Salt bridges and ion-dipole interactions are noncovalent; disulfide bonds are covalent.
  • A β-strand occupies an extended upper-left Ramachandran region, not the α-helical angles listed in the source table.
  • Quaternary structure is not inherently stronger than tertiary structure.
  • Protein concentration, purity, identity, and activity answer different questions.
  • Mass spectrometry measures mass-to-charge ratios; chemical shifts belong to NMR.
  • Conventional BCA uses 562 nm; exact assay settings should follow the chosen reagent instructions.
  • Crystals are often cryocooled, but freezing is not a universal requirement of X-ray crystallography.
  • Neither fixed mass cutoffs nor a universal R-free threshold should determine method choice or model quality.
Questions for active recall
  1. How would changing pH affect a charged side chain and an ion-exchange step?
  2. Why might adding a hydrogen bond fail to improve stability?
  3. How do φ, ψ, and χ describe different aspects of conformation?
  4. Why does diffraction intensity alone not supply an electron-density map?
  5. What does R-free test that R-work does not?
  6. How would you distinguish concentration from purity and activity?
  7. What evidence would justify advancing a sample to structure determination?
